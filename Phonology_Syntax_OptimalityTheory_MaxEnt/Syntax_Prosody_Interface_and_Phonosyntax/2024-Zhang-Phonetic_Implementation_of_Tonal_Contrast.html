The online processing of phonological contrasts in an L2: Mapping L1 allophones to L2 phonemes

Sijia Zhang, Molly Babel, Christopher Hammerly & Anne-Michelle Tessier — Laboratory Phonology: Journal of the Association for Laboratory Phonology, 17(1), 1–46 (2026). DOI: 10.16995/labphon.18470
The gist. A two-level dissection of L2 phonological difficulty: Mandarin L1 → English L2 learners were tested on English low vowel + nasal sequences (loVN: /æm æn æŋ ɑm ɑn ɑŋ/), where the vowels are allophones in Mandarin (low front vowel only before /n/, low back only before /ŋ/) but phonemes in English. Result: learners discriminate the contrasts fine at the phonetic level (AX task accuracy ~90–99%), but in a visual-world eye-tracking word-recognition task they show extra lexical competition relative to native listeners — and this competition is symmetric across L1-licit and L1-illicit sequences, indicating learners have effectively re-purposed their L1 allophones as L2 phonemes (allophones, not phonemes, are the mappable unit). A larger receptive vocabulary (LexTALE) reduced competition for both L1 and L2 listeners — "the lexicon feeds the phonology."

What the paper does

Experiment 1 (perception): speeded AX discrimination + perceptual similarity ratings on loVN pairs, three listener groups (English L1; Mandarin L1 in Canada; Mandarin L1 in China). Experiment 2 (lexical processing): visual-world eye-tracking with printed words; target/competitor pairs differed in the nasal (e.g. context [kɑn…] vs conquer [kɑŋ…]), in three conditions crossing whether the target/competitor sequences are licit or illicit in Mandarin, plus a baseline with identical licit sequences and onset-overlap fillers. Cluster-based permutation analysis (CPA) over time-binned fixations.

Key findings

  • Phonetic level: fine. AX accuracy was high for all groups (~90–99%); Mandarin listeners were slightly slower and slightly worse on nasal-different pairs (coda nasals have weak cues generally), but nothing catastrophic. Similarity ratings showed no "allophony blindness" — L2 listeners even rated vowel-different pairs as more distinct (possibly an allophone "pop-out" effect, cf. Weber 2001).
  • Lexical level: fuzzy. In eye-tracking, Mandarin listeners showed a significant, late (680–980ms) competition effect vs English listeners on loVN words, while phonologically-overlapping fillers showed no group difference — the difficulty is specific to loVN encoding, not general L2 delay.
  • Symmetric competition. No reliable asymmetry between "target licit / competitor illicit" and the reverse (contra the Weber & Cutler 2004 asymmetric-mapping pattern) — consistent with allophonic remapping (SLM-r; German et al. 2013) rather than dominant-category mapping.
  • Vocabulary size helps everyone. Higher LexTALE predicted less competition in both groups (with the effect slightly later for L2ers) — "phonological knowledge generalised across larger lexicons resolves competition more quickly" (echoing Llompart 2021; Daidone & Darcy 2021).

Interpretation & caveats

The authors locate the L2 difficulty at the phono-lexical level, not the phonetic level (Darcy et al. 2013; Turner 2022), consistent with the "fuzzy lexical representation" hypothesis (Gor 2018). They hedge on whether the fuzziness is specifically about the nasals or the vowels, note orthography (pinyin vs English spelling) as a possible contributor, and acknowledge the alternative reading that learners simply haven't encoded a nasal-place contrast at all — which they argue against given proficiency levels and the online use of the nasal cue. Caveats: printed-word displays, one contrast family, moderate sample sizes.

Relevance to Calliope-TS

Poetry-scansion is a native-like, literacy-heavy activity; but this paper matters for Calliope for three concrete reasons:

  • Insight: dictionary entries are phonolexical, and fuzziness is gradient. Calliope's lexicon assigns one (or several) pronunciations per word. This paper is a reminder that a listener/reader's representation of a word's form is a distribution, with weight on the forms they've actually heard — relevant to how Calliope handles pronunciation variants (e.g. stress doublets, reduced forms like 'tis), and to the general principle that variant lists should be weighted, not flat.
  • Insight: the level of the unit matters — allophones vs phonemes. When Calliope's OOV machinery decomposes words or resolves elisions (heav'n, o'er, th'expense), it is effectively deciding the mapping level (orthographic → phonemic → allophonic). The paper's evidence that allophones are the mappable, re-usable unit supports the toolkit's practice of keeping fine-grained (allophonic, articulatorily informed) representations available under the phonemic surface — e.g. the CMU stress-detail columns and IPA-level phones.
  • Insight: lexical knowledge sharpens phonological encoding. The vocabulary-size effect is an argument for frequency/lexicon-driven confidence in stress and phonology: for high-frequency, well-attested words the system should trust lexical entries; for low-frequency ones it should hedge toward rules — and for poetry, rare forms are the norm, so rule-fallback quality (English Stress Rule, suffix tables) is a first-class feature, not a corner case.
  • Methodological echo for evaluation: the visual-world paradigm's "competition" metric is a subtle way to evaluate phonological distinctiveness — an analogy for testing whether Calliope's rhyme/meter machinery actually keeps minimal pairs distinct (e.g. that ran/ram/rang-type contrasts survive the pipeline into the stress keys).

Key named insights

  1. Allophones as the mappable unit: L1 allophones can be systematically re-purposed as L2 phonemes — representation-level flexibility with a defined unit.
  2. Phonetic-lexical dissociation: perception can be intact while lexical encoding is fuzzy — the two levels must be modelled (and evaluated) separately.
  3. Symmetric competition: absence of the classic asymmetric-mapping signature indicates remapping, not defaulting to a dominant L1 category.
  4. Lexicon→phonology feedback: larger receptive vocabularies sharpen phono-lexical encoding for natives and learners alike — the lexicon is an active part of the phonological system.
