Prosodic effects of focus and constituency in Mandarin and in English

Wei Zhang, Meghan Clayards & Michael Wagner — Laboratory Phonology: Journal of the Association for Laboratory Phonology, 15(1), 1–45 (2024). DOI: 10.16995/labphon.9704
The gist. A matched production study (17 speakers per language) crossing focus position (A/B/C/wide) with constituent structure ([A or B] and C vs A or [B and C]) in coordinate name sequences, comparing English (a "stress-based" focus language) with Mandarin (a "pitch-range-based" one). Two headline results: (1) focus does not erase post-focal phrasing cues — syntactically-motivated phrasing distinctions survive after an early focus in both languages (constituency classification accuracy is high even when initial focus is correctly realised), supporting an orthogonal/additive model of prominence and phrasing; (2) the languages differ mainly in cue weighting, not kind: Mandarin relies more on f0 (and shows more extreme post-focal f0 compression), English more on duration; Mandarin lexical tone (T1 vs T4) modulates focus realisation, English stress pattern (initial vs final) modulates it. Intensity was a reliable phrasing cue in both languages.

What the paper does

Participants produced answers like "Not X or Y and Z who went…" in dialogue contexts where figures conveyed the intended bracketing (no commas — avoiding the direct-phrasing confound of earlier reading tasks, contra Wagner & McAuliffe 2019). Eight syllables per utterance; duration, max f0, mean intensity extracted per syllable. Analysis via (a) random-forest classifiers (which cues best classify focus/constituency, and whether constituency survives post-focally), and (b) linear mixed models testing focus×constituency interactions per cue per syllable.

Key findings

  • No focus×constituency interaction in either language (LMMs: the interaction was non-significant in ~all models; one isolated Mandarin duration effect didn't replicate in combined-language models). Phrasing cues persist post-focally: constituency accuracy was higher, not lower, when initial focus was accurately classified — i.e. the phrasing signal wasn't traded off against the focus signal.
  • Cue-weight differences: focus classification was driven most by f0 in Mandarin (65.9% accuracy) and by duration in English (51.3%); post-focal f0 compression was far larger in Mandarin (−31 to −44 Hz extra lowering) — consistent with the "pitch-range-based" typology, but only as a matter of degree, since both languages used all cues.
  • Phrasing cues: pre-boundary syllables were longer and lower in intensity in both languages (2-syllable pre-boundary lengthening window; intensity lowering confined to the last syllable); the coordinator words themselves carried strong phrasing information in Mandarin; f0 played little consistent role in boundary marking (list-intonation confounds in English).
  • Focus-vs-phrase-prominence overlap: B-focus (the middle, phrase-internal name) was the hardest to classify — focused B was acoustically confusable with phrase-final prominence, illustrating that lengthening is an overlapped cue for both dimensions (the "cue-anti-correlation" story from Wagner 2022).
  • Lexical prosody interacts: T4 (falling tone) names used f0 more than T1 for focus; final-stressed English names concentrated focus cues on the stressed syllable.

Interpretation & caveats

Results favour theories that keep prominence and phrasing representationally separate (Wagner & McAuliffe 2019; additive prosody models) over accounts predicting post-focal neutralisation of phrasing. Caveats: English data collected online vs Mandarin in person; Mandarin second syllables were a controlled honorific while English ones varied; only coordinate structures tested; reading-like (though context-driven) speech.

Relevance to Calliope-TS

This is one of the two most directly pertinent papers in this set for Calliope (the other being the Moghiseh/Sonderegger/Wagner ITL paper), because it is Wagner's own lab validating, cross-linguistically, the two-dimensional architecture Calliope already implements:

  • Insight: prominence and phrasing must stay separate axes. Calliope already keeps phraseStress (an integer ranking) separate from the κ/ϕ/ι hierarchy. This paper is strong empirical backing for that split: focus (prominence) and constituency (phrasing) are orthogonal in production, so the pipeline is right to compute them in separate stages and combine additively — and the interaction terms Calliope implicitly assumes (e.g. post-focal compression affecting key-stress strength) should be treated as second-order, not first-order.
  • Insight: the "beginnings free, endings strict" logic is consistent with post-focal preservation of phrasing. Key stresses at right edges of IU/PP remain valid evidence even after an early focus — the meter layer may rely on them without fearing erasure.
  • Insight: cue-weighting by language. For the Russian side of Calliope (a pitch/accent-based system) and any future languages, the paper supports language-specific cue profiles (f0-heavy vs duration-heavy) — and warns that the difference is gradient, so "stress-based vs pitch-range-based" should be a parameter, not a binary flag. The Russian pipeline's reliance on accentology (stress dictionaries + neural accentuator) is exactly the kind of f0/register-cue system this paper describes.
  • Insight: lexical prosody modulates focus realisation. Tone type (Mandarin) and stress position (English) both modulate how focus is acoustically realised — a reminder that lexical strata (Calliope's CMU-derived contours) legitimately feed back into phrase-level decisions, and that tone-language support can't simply borrow English focus rules.
  • Methodological transfer: random-forest feature-importance analysis is a ready-made diagnostic for Calliope's own rule weights — e.g. measuring which constraints actually carry the information in the metrical fitter's decision (a "which cues matter" audit analogous to the paper's per-language cue ranking).

Key named insights

  1. Post-focal phrasing survives: focus does not neutralise syntactically-motivated phrasing distinctions, in stress-based or pitch-range-based languages alike.
  2. Orthogonality by design: prominence and phrasing are additively encoded; cue overlap (lengthening) is the main source of local ambiguity, resolved by cue anti-correlation.
  3. Gradient typology: "stress-based vs pitch-range-based" is a difference in cue weights and post-focal compression depth, not a categorical split.
  4. Tone/stress interplay: lexical tone and stress position shape how focus cues are deployed — lexical phonology leaks into phrase prosody in systematic ways.
