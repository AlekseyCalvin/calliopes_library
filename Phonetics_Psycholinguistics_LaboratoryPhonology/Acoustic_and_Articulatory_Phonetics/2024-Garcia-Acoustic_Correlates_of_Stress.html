Weight effects and the role of the foot: English versus Portuguese

Guilherme D. Garcia & Heather Goad — Laboratory Phonology: Journal of the Association for Laboratory Phonology, 15(1), 1–37 (2024). DOI: 10.16995/labphon.6420
The gist. English and Brazilian Portuguese look like they have nearly identical stress systems (weight-sensitive, final/penult/antepenult windows), but this paper argues they are formally different: English is genuinely foot-based (binary moraic trochees), Portuguese may not build feet at all. Three converging arguments: (1) experimental — in forced-choice stress-preference tasks on nonce words, English speakers show a neutral weight effect in antepenultimate position (a heavy antepenult neither attracts nor repels stress), exactly as a moraic-trochee grammar predicts, while Portuguese speakers show a positive weight effect there (heavy antepenult attracts stress), which no foot-based parse can produce cleanly; (2) sonority — sonorant-vs-obstruent coda effects appear in Portuguese antepenults but not English; (3) word minimality — English lexical words, truncations, and hypocoristics are never monomoraic (bee, bit ok; *bɪ banned), while Portuguese freely tolerates subminimal CV words and monomoraic hypocoristics (Fé from Fernanda). Conclusion: the foot is not a universal prosodic constituent; lexical stress and foot structure are formally independent.

What the paper does

Two forced-choice nonce-word judgment tasks (26 Portuguese, 25 English speakers; minimal pairs differing only in stress location) crossing weight profile (LLL, HLL, LHL) with stress position (antepenult vs penult), plus a lexicon/minimality survey of CV words, truncation, and hypocorisation in both languages. Bayesian hierarchical logistic regressions (brms) with ROPE-based null-effect assessment.

Key findings

  • Antepenultimate weight: English β(HLL) ≈ −0.09 (95% CrI includes 0 — null effect, as predicted by moraic trochees + final extrametricality); Portuguese β(HLL) = +0.34 (credibly positive — heavy antepenults attract stress, which the foot-based parse cannot accommodate without marked unparsed syllables or uneven trochees).
  • Penultimate weight: positive in both languages (heavy penult attracts stress) — the surface similarity.
  • Sonority: coda sonority modulated Portuguese antepenultimate preferences (β = 0.40) but not English ones — a fine-grained reflex of the same split.
  • Minimality: English bans monomoraic lexical words and monomoraic truncations/hypocoristics (foot binarity); Portuguese allows them productively — its minimal-word condition references the syllable (CV), not the foot.
  • Portuguese truncation/hypocorisation produces both iambs and trochees unpredictably, further weakening foot-based analyses.

Interpretation & caveats

The authors propose a two-parameter typology: foot present/absent × lexical stress present/absent (English: foot+stress; Portuguese: stress, foot?; Québec French: foot, no stress; Turkish: neither). For footless languages, stress windows can be captured by anti-lapse/grid constraints (Gordon 2002) — with learnability rescuing the "midpoint pathology" problem (Stanton 2016). They adopt weighted (HG/MaxEnt-style) constraints rather than strict rankings. Caveats: nonce-word judgments, limited coda set, no articulation data; the Portuguese-footless claim is offered as the best interpretation of a challenging dataset, not a settled result.

Relevance to Calliope-TS

This paper is a direct theoretical probe of the load-bearing assumptions in Calliope's lexical-stress and OOV stages, which build on Hayes-style English stress (quantity-sensitive, extrametricality, moraic trochees) and on the CMU dictionary's weight columns:

  • Insight: weight effects are positionally gated in foot-based languages. Calliope's English OOV stress rule already implements exactly this shape (heavy penult attracts; antepenult default otherwise; pre-stressing suffixes). This paper provides clean experimental confirmation that the gating (weight works in penult, not antepenult) is the signature of footing — and that adding "weight attracts stress everywhere" (as in Portuguese) would be a different, non-foot grammar. The kit's English rules are on the right side of this divide; the Portuguese observation matters if Calliope ever gains a Romance language.
  • Insight: weight ≠ feet ≠ stress. The paper's three-way decoupling (foot / lexical stress / minimality) maps onto Calliope's separable stages: lexical contour (dictionary), foot-level meter fitting (DP over metrical templates), and word-minimality constraints (relevant to truncation/hypocorisation handling in poetic diction and to "minimal word" judgments in rhyme/composition tools).
  • Insight: gradient/weighted constraints over ranked ones. The authors' preference for weighted constraints (Goldwater & Johnson 2003 MaxEnt lineage) is precisely Calliope's own MaxEnt meter-fitting architecture — mutual reinforcement that weighted, learnable constraint systems are the right computational frame.
  • Insight: sonority is real, and language-specific. Coda-sonority effects on stress preference (Portuguese) vs none (English) argue for keeping sonority-weighted rules out of the English default while retaining the hooks for other languages.
  • Note for the web app's meter-identification: the paper's nonce-word methodology is a template for testing whether the kit's own stress rules generalise — the same "does the grammar generalise the right way to novel forms?" question Calliope's OOV fallback must answer.

Key named insights

  1. Positional weight-gating: foot-based English shows weight effects in penultimate but not antepenultimate position — a diagnostic signature, experimentally confirmed.
  2. Foot-independence: lexical stress can exist without feet (Portuguese), and feet without lexical stress (Québec French); the foot is not universal.
  3. Syllable-vs-foot minimality: minimal-word constraints can reference the syllable (Portuguese CV) rather than the foot (English bimoraic) — diagnostic of the underlying prosodic inventory.
  4. Weighted-constraint frame: gradient HG/MaxEnt constraint weights capture the Portuguese pattern better than ranked OT — validation of the MaxEnt approach Calliope already uses.
