Phonetic correlates of prefixedness in four varieties of English

Sarah Hawkins, Jen Hay, Rachel Smith, Jennifer Stuart-Smith & Robert Fromont — Laboratory Phonology: Journal of the Association for Laboratory Phonology, 17(1), 1–64 (2026). DOI: 10.16995/labphon.17571
The gist. Does morphology leak into phonetics in spontaneous speech, across dialects? Yes. Using 1002 tokens of dis-/mis- words from conversational corpora in four varieties (New Zealand, Ohio US, Glasgow, Liverpool), 14 acoustic measures were reduced via PCA to five dimensions ([s] duration/peakiness, [s] spectral balance, vowel duration, vowel quality/F2−F1, [s] low-frequency cutoff). Every dialect marks prefix status with at least one dimension; vowel duration is the most robust and generalisable cue (prefixes have longer vowels in NZ, US, and Liverpool — but not Glasgow, whose KIT-vowel phonology constrains it); and the effect is not a word-frequency artefact. The binary prefix/nonprefix distinction dominates, but within prefixes, gradient "felt prefixedness" (from an online rating task, highly consistent across dialect groups, rs ≈ .9–.94) predicts vowel quality and [s] cutoff — morphology is gradient in production, and each dialect "solves" the prefix cue in its own way, tied to its dialect-specific reduction patterns and KIT-vowel quality.

What the paper does

Corpus phonetics: forced-aligned conversational speech (Buckeye US; NZ archives; Glasgow; Liverpool), words beginning with /dɪs/ or /mɪs/ classified as prefixed (388) or unprefixed (614) by semantic criteria (free stem + transparent relation + consistent prefix meaning); 78 online raters provided continuous "how prefixed?" judgments for 214 word types; 14 acoustic measures per token → permutation-tested PCA (5 components, 74% variance) → mixed-effects regressions with binary PrefixStatus and gradient Rating, controlling word frequency, stress position, boundary type (4th phoneme stop = coda possible), mis/dis, corpus.

Key findings

  • All five PCs show a significant binary prefixedness effect (in isolation or interaction). PC3 (vowel duration) is the most robust: prefixes longer in US/NZ/Liverpool, not Glasgow; stronger when the following phoneme is a stop (CodaPossible). PC1 ([s] duration/peakiness) differentiates only in NZ; PC2 ([s] spectral balance) interacts with stress; PC4 (vowel quality) differentiates in CodaPossible words (more canonical KIT for prefixes — toward schwa-like [ɪ] in NZ/Glasgow, toward [ɪ] in Ohio); PC5 ([s] cutoff) shows the only main effect independent of other predictors.
  • Not frequency: word frequency affected vowel duration, but prefix status survived frequency as a controlled factor everywhere — confirming Smith et al. (2012) in messy real speech.
  • Binary dominates; gradience exists within prefixes: ratings were bimodal (strongly categorical), but within the prefixed class, higher felt-prefixedness predicted more canonical vowel quality (PC4) and higher [s] cutoff (PC5). Morphologically "ambiguous" words (e.g. misnomer) sat in the middle of the rating scale, and the acoustics followed the gradient.
  • Dialectal solutions differ: each variety uses a different combination of dimensions — the effect is real everywhere, but its phonetic "solution" is tied to the dialect's reduction phonology and KIT quality (Glasgow's vowel duration is constrained by other morphophonological factors — Rathcke & Stuart-Smith 2016).

Interpretation & caveats

The prefix carries "a heavier beat" than the identical nonprefix syllable (Raffelsiefen 1999's prosodic-word boundary) — and dialects implement "heavier beat" via whatever their reduction system affords. The authors connect this to the communicative function (prefixes carry negation/reversal meaning; listeners use the first-syllable detail online to predict word identity — Clayards et al. 2021) and to a Linear Discriminative Learning (LDL) frame where form-meaning patterns are learned probabilistically. Caveats: forced-alignment noise (hand-correction didn't change results), unequal corpora sizes, mostly NZ-heavy token counts, speech-style differences across corpora.

Relevance to Calliope-TS

Morphology is everywhere in poetry — and prefixes are the exact stratum where Calliope's lexical (CMU) stage, POS/parse stage, and stress machinery interact:

  • Insight: morphological structure should modulate stress keys. The paper shows prefix vs nonprefix affects syllable prominence-phonetics systematically (discolour vs discover differ in vowel duration and quality). Calliope's tier system could encode this as a small, principled prominence bonus for morpheme-boundary syllables (a "prefix boundary ⇒ slightly stronger weak syllable" effect) — exactly the kind of morphology→phonology mapping the toolkit's morphology column in Nounsing-Pro already hints at (suffixType/prefixType), and a natural companion to the existing NOMD/compound logic.
  • Insight: gradient morphology = gradient phonetics. Felt-prefixedness (transparency, stem freedom) varies continuously and the acoustics follow. For Calliope's OOV morphology (decomposeStress, suffix/prefix tables), this argues for confidence-weighted morphological parses: transparent, productive affixation (mis- + free stem) should be trusted more than opaque spellings (mystery, disaster) — a principled way to decide between morphological decomposition and whole-word lexical lookup, which is precisely the decision Calliope's OOV fallback makes today with hard rules.
  • Insight: dialect parameterisation of reduction. The dialect-specific cue solutions (and the Glasgow exception) are a caution against hard-coding "reduction = centralisation toward schwa" globally — relevant to Calliope's handling of non-US poetry (the user's own corpus work spans British and World English), and to the toolkit's doctrine of not essentialising one dialect's prosody.
  • Insight: frequency ≠ morphology. The paper cleanly separates word-frequency reduction from morphological enhancement — the same two confounds Calliope faces when choosing between frequency-weighted (Zipf) handling in the lexicon and structural rules; the finding that morphology survives frequency control supports keeping structural rules independent of frequency gating.
  • Insight: first-syllable detail predicts word identity. Listeners use prefix detail online (Clayards et al. 2021). For the reading/teaching use case of Calliope, this means the tool's morpheme-aware display (e.g. showing the mis-/dis- boundary) is not decoration — it matches the perceptual strategy of real listeners.

Key named insights

  1. Morphology is audible: prefix status has systematic phonetic correlates in spontaneous speech, across four very different dialects.
  2. Vowel duration as the workhorse: the most robust prefix cue is vowel duration (with the Glasgow exception) — again duration proves the most reliable prominence-adjacent signal.
  3. Gradient affixedness: within the prefixed class, "felt prefixedness" grades the phonetics continuously — transparency is a gradient with acoustic reflexes.
  4. Dialectal cue solutions: varieties solve the same morphological-prominence problem with different phonetic dimensions, tied to their reduction phonology.
