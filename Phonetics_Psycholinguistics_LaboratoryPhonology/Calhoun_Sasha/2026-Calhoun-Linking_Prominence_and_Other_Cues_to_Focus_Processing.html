Linking prominence, and other cues, to the processing of focus across languages

Sasha Calhoun — Laboratory Phonology: Journal of the Association for Laboratory Phonology, 17(1), 1–29 (2026). DOI: 10.16995/labphon.18927
The gist. A synthesis/review from the listener's side of focus processing. Focus (Krifka: "focus indicates the presence of alternatives") is signalled by an astonishing range of cues — contrastive prosodic prominence, syntactic clefts and word order, grammatical role, verb semantics, gesture, fine phonetic detail — and listeners weight these cues differently across languages (English: prominence and clefting roughly equal; Samoan: clefting dominates; Mandarin: prominence dominates; L2 listeners re-weight their L1 pattern). Psycholinguistically, focus-marking selectively activates the focused word's alternative set: priming studies show alternatives activated (or non-alternatives suppressed), eye-tracking shows anticipatory looks to contrastive referents, and memory studies show focus strengthens encoding of mentioned alternatives. The proposed unifying view: focus is an attentional tool; prominence is "more highlighted than expected" given probabilistic expectations over all cues — a processing-instruction to attend, activate alternatives, and resolve them against the discourse.

What the paper does

Reviews the Rooth/alternative-semantics + QUD (question-under-discussion) theory of focus; distinguishes phonological (stress/register/phrase-based) from phonetic prominence (Kügler & Calhoun 2020); then surveys her group's cross-linguistic experiments: lexical priming of alternatives in English/Mandarin (Calhoun, Yan & White 2025; Yan, Calhoun & Warren 2023); probe-recognition and delayed-recall alternative-memory tasks in Samoan/German/Vietnamese; focus-judgment tasks pitting prominence vs clefting cues in English, Samoan, Mandarin, and L1-Mandarin L2-English; false-alternative processing tasks showing a default object-focus bias; Spanish intransitive production showing verb-semantics effects (unaccusatives shift accent to subject); Turkish gesture-synchrony production data.

Key findings

  • Focus activates alternatives: in English and Mandarin, contrastive prominence selectively primed identical/alternative targets vs non-contrastive associates; the pattern differed subtly by language (activation vs suppression accounts) and by grammatical role (broad alternative sets primed for objects, not subjects — subjects are prototypically given).
  • Focus strengthens memory for alternatives: delayed-recognition results (Samoan clefts; German focus particles; Vietnamese prominence) show mentioned alternatives are better recalled/verified under focus — but immediate probe-recognition can show the opposite (harder to reject unmentioned alternatives while the set is hot), reflecting the same activation.
  • Cue weighting is language-specific: English — prominence and clefting both read as focus cues (mismatches weaken judgments); Samoan — clefting outweighs prominence; Mandarin — prominence outweighs clefting; L2 (Mandarin→English) learners reversed toward the L1-typical weighting, with residual prominence sensitivity. Default object-focus bias (English + Mandarin) is so strong it drives processing even when the object is not overtly marked.
  • Multimodal and fine-phonetic cues: gestures (head/eyebrow; Turkish data: focused phrases gestured 68% vs 27% topic vs 5% background, with near-perfect prosody-gesture synchrony at nuclear phrases); segmental detail (Korean nasalisation reduction under focus; German listeners use lack of reduction as a focus cue — Mitterer, Kim & Cho 2024).
  • Prominence is relative and probabilistic: perception of one word's prominence depends on the rest of the phrase and on expectations from the discourse; H* vs L+H* interpretative domains overlap (Watson, Tanenhaus & Gunlogson 2008).

Interpretation & caveats

Calhoun's synthesis: prominence = deviation from expected prominence given all cues and context; focus = a probabilistic processing instruction to treat the constituent as attended, generate and resolve its alternative set. This unifies production cue diversity with processing consequences and cross-linguistic cue-weight variation. Caveats acknowledged: most processing work is on Germanic languages; perception studies cover a small cue subset; multilingual/heritage speakers may show novel weightings; individual differences (neurodiversity, empathy, pragmatic skill) modulate prominence perception.

Relevance to Calliope-TS

This is arguably the single most pipeline-relevant review in this set, because Calliope's phrase-stress stage already encodes Wagner/Krifka focus machinery, and Calhoun's framework tells you what the output of that stage should mean and how to weight its evidence:

  • Insight: prominence is expectedness, not strength. Calhoun's "more prominent than expected" framing suggests Calliope's phrase-stress stage should compute, alongside the absolute integer ranking, an expectedness prior (default nuclear = final/object position; subject focus = marked). The toolkit already has the seeds — the default-object-bias is implicit in Wagner's NSR right-branching — but making it an explicit prior would let the deviation from default drive focus interpretation, exactly as listeners do (and exactly as the false-alternatives experiments show: objects are processed as focused even unmarked).
  • Insight: alternative sets are the semantic payload. Calliope's focusAssociateOf (just/only/even/merely) and its contrastive-focus predicates could be extended into an alternative-generation layer: when a word is focus-marked, the "meaning" the tool reports (for the synopsis/teaching use case) is its alternative set — semantically plausible alternatives from the stanza (rhyme-fellows, coordinate partners, repeated lemmas). This connects focus machinery directly to the rhyme/continuity passes (rhymeFocal, discourseGiven) and to the semantic ambitions of the kit.
  • Insight: cues are weighted, not summed blindly. The English/Samoan/Mandarin cue-weight differences argue for language-parameterised cue weights in the phrasal stage (prominence-cue weight vs syntactic-cue weight), and for a conflict-resolution policy when prominence and syntax disagree — English follows a compromise, Samoan follows syntax, Mandarin follows prominence. Calliope currently resolves such conflicts with fixed decision tables (combineDecision); a weight parameter would be a principled upgrade and matches the kit's MaxEnt philosophy.
  • Insight: the object-focus default should modulate key stresses. Because phrase-final/object position is the default focus position, the meter fitter's right-edge evidence (key stresses) is, in information-structural terms, evidence about expected prominence — consistent with the "endings strict" principle and with the fact that line-final nuclear position is metrically decisive in English verse.
  • Insight: multimodal/gestural cues are outside text, but their existence matters. For a text-based tool, the lesson is humility: the text underdetermines focus; the tool should expose focus ambiguity (e.g. near-tie prominence in the ranking) rather than hide it — Calhoun's work shows listeners resolve it probabilistically with context the text alone doesn't carry.
  • Insight: givenness machinery validated. The memory/anticipation findings (focus strengthens alternatives; givenness de-accenting is categorical) support Calliope's stanza-level discourseGiven/rhymeFocal demotion logic — and suggest it could be stronger: given words are the most reliably de-accented class in production and processing data.

Key named insights

  1. Focus-as-attention: focus is a processing instruction to attend and resolve alternatives — not merely a prominence flag.
  2. Expectedness-based prominence: prominence is deviation from probabilistic expectations over all cues and context.
  3. Language-specific cue weighting: prominence vs syntax cue weights differ (English balanced, Samoan syntax-dominant, Mandarin prominence-dominant) and re-weight in L2.
  4. Default object-focus bias: objects are processed as focused by default — the strongest, most cross-linguistic "default prominence" fact in this literature.
