<center><font size=+2>English Stress</font></center>

<p><b>1. Historical landmarks</b>
<p>Gill (1619). Cap. XXV-XXVI.
<p>Steele (1775) distinguished three levels of stress.
<p>Jespersen (1909/1954):
<blockquote>Mr. William Archer, after a long list of seemingly arbitrary
accentuations in the English language (<i>America To-Day</i>, p. 193),
goes on to say: `But the larger our list of examples, the more capricious
does our accentuation seem, the more evidently subject to mere accidents
of fashion. There is scarcely a trace of consistent or rational principle
in the matter.' It will be the object of the following pages to show that
there <i>are</i> principles, and that the `capriciousness' is merely the
natural consequences of the fact that there is not <i>one</i> single principle,
but several principles working sometimes against each other. (p. 160)</blockquote>
Kingdon (1958) distinguished a) "Romanic-type compounds" (derived Latinate
words) b) "Greek-type compounds" (like lexical compounds) c) "English-type
compounds" (lexical compounds).
<p>Chomsky, Halle &amp; Lukoff (1956), (summary account in Chomsky and
Miller 1963):
<blockquote>These rules ... are ordered, and apply in a <i>cycle,</i> first
to the smallest constituents (that is, lexical morphemes), then to the
next larger ones, and so on, until the largest domain of phonetic processes
is reached .... essentiallly the same rules apply both inside and outside
the word. Thus ... a single cycle of transformational rules ... by repeated
application, determines the phonetic structure of a complex form.</blockquote>
a) A <i>substantive</i> rule that assigns stress in initial position in
nouns (also stems) under very general circumstances. [Germanic stress].
<br>b) A <i>nuclear stress </i>rule that makes the last main stress dominant,
thus weakening all other stresses in the construction.
<br>c) The <i>vowel reduction </i>rule.
<br>d) A rule of <i>stress adjustment </i>that weakens all nonmain<b> </b>stresses
in a word by one.
<p><b>2. Chomsky &amp; Halle (1968)</b>, especially chs. 2-3. The fulfillment
of their concerted effort to determine a complete set of rules for English
phonology, dominated by stress assignment and its consequences.
<p><b>2.1. Stress placement is sensitive to [syllable] weight</b>
<p>p. 29 weak cluster - "a string consisting of a simple vocalic nucleus
followed by no more than one consonant".
<p>strong cluster - "a string consisting of either a vocalic nucleus followed
by two or more consonants or a complex vocalic nucleus followed by any
number of consonants." (i.e. light vs. heavy rimes).
<p><b>2.2. Stress rules are sensitive to lexical categories</b>
<p>(21) <i>Main Stress Rule</i>
<p>V -> [1 stress] / X - C<font size=-2>0</font>]<font size=-2>NAV</font>
<p>Rule (21) assigns primary stress to the final vowel of the word, e.g.
<i>ev&aacute;de</i>,
<i>supr&eacute;me</i>, <i>ex&iacute;st</i>, <i>abs&uacute;rd</i>, all of
which end in a strong cluster. If a verb or adjective has a final weak
cluster, the stress is placed on the penultimate syllable e.g. <i>r&eacute;lish</i>,
<i>c&oacute;vet</i>,
<i>dev&eacute;lop</i>, <i>st&oacute;lid</i>, <i>c&oacute;mmon</i>,
<i>cland&eacute;stine</i>.
<p><b>2.3. Sensitivity to the Latinate/Germanic distinction</b>
<p><font face="Times">a) Germanic affixes don't affect stress placement,
e.g. &eacute;arth, &eacute;arthly, un&eacute;arthly, un&eacute;arthliness.</font>
<br><font face="Times">b) Latinate suffixes may affect stress placement,
e.g. t&eacute;mpest, temp&eacute;stuous, tempestu&oacute;sity.</font>
<br><font face="Times">c) Stress may shift onto Latinate prefixes, e.g.
inv&eacute;st vs. </font>&iacute;<font face="Times">nverse, c&agrave;tat&oacute;nic
vs. catastrophe.</font>
<br><font face="Times">d) Stress never shifts onto Germanic prefixes, e.g.
&ograve;ver[c&oacute;ok] - no forms like ov&eacute;ric [o<font size=-1>U</font></font><font face="Font220">v<img SRC="phon104.gif" height=14 width=3><img SRC="epsilon0.gif" height=8 width=8>rIk</font><font face="Times">]</font>

<p><b>2.5. Stress subordination</b>
<p>p. 64 "The rules that determine stress contours are, for the most part,
rules that assign primary stress in certain positions, at the same time
weakening the stresses in all other positions".
<p><b>2.5. Alternating stress rule (p.78) </b>(for secondary stresses)
<p>V -> [1 stress] / - C<font size=-2>0</font> V C<font size=-2>0</font>
V<font size=-2>1</font> C<font size=-2>0</font>]
<p>hurricAn => hurricA<sup><font size=-2>1</font></sup>n [Main stress rule]
<br>=> hu<sup><font size=-2>1</font></sup>rricA<sup><font size=-2>1</font></sup>n
[Alternating stress rule]
<br>=> hu<sup><font size=-2>1</font></sup>rricA<sup><font size=-2>2</font></sup>n
[Stress subordination]
<p><b>3. Liberman and Prince (1977)</b>
<p>"certain features of prosodic systems like that of English, in particular
the phenomenon of `stress subordination', are not to be referred primarily
to the properties of individual segments (or syllables), but rather reflect
a hierarchical rhythmic structuring that organizes the syllables, words,
and syntactic phrases of a sentence."

<p><b>Digression on Iambic Reversal / Rhythm Rule / Stress Retraction</b>
<p>SPE and Metrical Phonology both capture the <i>preservation of relative
prominence under embedding</i>. But there are counter-cases,
<br>e.g.:
<p>e.g. <i>thirt&eacute;en</i> vs. <i>th&igrave;rteen m&eacute;n</i>, <i>&agrave;chrom&aacute;tic</i>
vs. <i>&aacute;chrom&agrave;tic l&ecirc;ns</i>

of which the appropriate stress configurations are marked as `clashing',
thus producing a pressure for change."

is not redrawn by the reapplication of [foot construction]."
<p><b>5. Hayes (1982)</b>: extrametricality
<p>i) The final syllable is extrametrical in nouns and suffixed adjectives,
e.g. <i>serendipi&lt;ty>, sensa&lt;tion>, perso&lt;nal>. </i>With this
proviso, antepenultimate stress is eliminated. Word final syllables are
stressed if heavy, otherwise stress is penultimate (modulo extrametricality).
<p>ii) The final consonant is extrametrical in underived verbs and adjectives,
e.g. <i>soli&lt;d>, supre&lt;me>.</i>
<p><b>6. English stress parameters</b>
<p>As work in metrical theory progressed and was extended to many languages,
our conception of English stress assignment became embedded in, and was
constrained by, a <i>parametric</i> view of options for metrical structure
(Halle and Vergnaud 1987, Booij 1983), taking a lead from Chomskyan syntax.
<p>1) Principles: Words consist of feet and feet consist of syllables.
<p>2) Parameter: In English the <i>rightmost </i>foot is strongest (domain
of main stress) e(r&aacute;se), i(r&aacute;te), mu(t&aacute;tion), (&egrave;cu)(m&eacute;ni)&lt;cal>,
(&agrave;nti)(d&igrave;se)(st&agrave;blish)&lt;men>(t&aacute;ri)&lt;an>.
Cf. Russian: leftmost - (&uacute;<img SRC="phon96.gif" height=18 width=7>a)(s&agrave;m).
<p>3) Parameter: Bounded feet are <i>maximally binary</i>; ternary feet
are dealt with by extrametricality, and can only ocur at the edges of words
(or cycles). Thus: (H&agrave;ma)(m&egrave;lid)(&aacute;nthe)&lt;mum>. Cf.
<i>unbounded </i>feet in Khalkha Mongolian (x<img SRC="phon104.gif" height=14 width=3>&ouml;tE<img SRC="epsilon0.gif" height=8 width=8>lb<img SRC="epsilon0.gif" height=8 width=8>r<img SRC="epsilon0.gif" height=8 width=8>)
`leadership', French (originalit&eacute;).
<p>4) Parameter: In words with an odd number of syllables (excepting extrametrical
ones), left-over syllables occur at the <i>beginning</i>; e.g. a(g&eacute;nda),
To(p&eacute;ka), a(r&iacute;se). In Maranungku they occur at the <i>end</i>
e.g. (l&aacute;ng<img SRC="phon56.gif" height=19 width=7>ka)(r&agrave;te)t&igrave;.
<p>5) Parameter: In English, non-tonic binary feet immediately
<i>precede</i>
the tonic foot. <i>Problem: (&agrave;bra)ca(d&aacute;bra).</i>
<p>6) Parameter: The <i>leftmost</i> syllable in a foot is strongest e.g.
(m&aacute;n), (m&aacute;nner), (m&aacute;na)&lt;ger>. Cf. Weri (k&ugrave;)(lip&uacute;),
(ul&ugrave;)(am&iacute;t), (&aacute;)(kun&egrave;)(tep&aacute;l), in which
the <i>rightmost</i> syllable is strongest.
<p>7) Parameter: Feet are quantity-sensitive i.e. a heavy syllable must
be the head of a foot. (Does not exclude possibility that light syllables
could be syllable heads too) e.g. (f&ugrave;nda)(m&eacute;ntal), (sp&iacute;ral)
but in(&eacute;rt).
<p>8) Paramter: Secondary stresses are placed from right to left on the
head of every foot other than the tonic foot. (Stress assignment is iterative.
Cf. Spanish, Polish.) e.g. (h&agrave;ma)(m&egrave;li)(d&aacute;nthe)&lt;mum>.
<p><b>7. The Abracadabra problem.</b>
<p>(&agrave;bra)ca(d&aacute;bra), Kalamazoo, Luxipalilla, Hardecanute,
okefenokee, Nebuchadnezzar, paraphernalia, Kilimanjaro: these words appear
to require either a medial extrametrical syllable or a ternary foot.
<p>Hammond's solution: secondary feet are built left to right. Following
Hammond's suggestion, see also Halle and Kenstowicz (1991) section 7, McCarthy
and Prince (1993) section 3.
<p><b>8. Phrasal stress</b>
<p>Roca and Johnson follow Chomsky and Halle (1968) in contrasting e.g.
lexical <i>bl&aacute;ckb&igrave;rd</i> vs. phrasal <i>bl&agrave;ck b&iacute;rd</i>.
But we cannot in general state that phrasal stress is right-headed. Noun-noun
sequences are <i>usually</i> left-headed (e.g. d&oacute;g-ho&ugrave;se),
but there are exceptions (e.g. l&ograve;bster rag&oacute;ut).
