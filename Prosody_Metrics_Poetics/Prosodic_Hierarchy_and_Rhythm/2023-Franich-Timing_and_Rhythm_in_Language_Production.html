How we speak when we speak to a beat: The influence of temporal coupling on phonetic enhancement

Kathryn Franich — Laboratory Phonology: Journal of the Association for Laboratory Phonology, 13(1), 1–51 (2022). DOI: 10.16995/labphon.6452
The gist. Why are stressed syllables phonetically "enhanced" (longer, more open, louder)? The standard answer is grammatical (prominence ⇒ enhancement rules). Franich tests an alternative: coordination itself causes enhancement. In a metronome synchronisation–continuation task, speakers of English (a stress language) and Medʉmba (a Grassfields Bantu tone language with positional prominence but no phonetic word stress) repeated words in-phase ("onbeat") or out-of-phase ("offbeat") with a beat. In both languages, syllables produced in time with the beat were longer and had higher F1 (more jaw lowering) — two classic stress correlates — while f0 and intensity showed only task-wide, non-coupling effects. The effect is anchored: it hit the syllables aligned with the beat, appeared even vs uncoordinated speech, and scaled like "stress in a syllable-timed language" for Medʉmba (10–13% lengthening vs English's ~40%). The argument: in-phase coordination is more stable (superimposition in coordination dynamics), and stability ⇒ amplitude ⇒ enhancement; diachronically, such coupling-driven lengthening may be the phonetic precursor that languages phonologise into stress.

What the paper does

Metronome synchronisation-continuation task: 12 English + 12 Medʉmba speakers repeated 22 words per language (stress-pattern-controlled English; tone-pattern-controlled Medʉmba) to a metronome in onbeat (in-phase) or offbeat (120° phase) alignment, then continued without the beat; an uncoordinated condition served as baseline. Only continuation-phase data were analysed (removing the confound of speaking over the metronome). Measures: vowel duration, f0, intensity, F1, F2, Euclidean distance from vowel-space centre, and metronome-alignment (p-centre-based) distance. Mixed models with phasing × position × stress/tone (+ vowel-height/backness) predictors.

Key findings

  • Duration: onbeat vowels were longer than offbeat — most strongly in word-initial position (English: initial-syllable effect; Medʉmba: overall +3.4ms, larger word-initially). Relative duration (stressed/unstressed ratio, English) was higher onbeat than both offbeat and no-beat — i.e. genuine enhancement, not just rate effects.
  • F1: higher (more open/central articulation) onbeat in both languages, again localised to beat-aligned syllables and low vowels especially — consistent with greater jaw displacement under stable in-phase coupling.
  • F0 and intensity: no coupling-specific effects (f0 rose onbeat in English but fell onbeat in Medʉmba — language-specific, not coupling; intensity was generally higher offbeat, arguably effort). These are the cues that don't show the effect — important negative results for the theory.
  • Alignment: English speakers anchored stressed syllables near the beat (within ~7ms); Medʉmba speakers anchored high tones and prominent stem-initial syllables; variability was lower onbeat (more stable coupling).
  • Cross-linguistic scale: the coupling-induced lengthening in Medʉmba (10–13%) resembles stress effects in syllable-timed languages (Spanish ~10%), suggesting the effect is a general motor property whose grammaticalisation into stress differs by language.

Interpretation & caveats

Franich interprets the results via coordination dynamics (Kelso; Schwartz et al. 1995): in-phase coupling is the "global attractor"; stability and amplitude covary (Kudo et al. 2006); speech articulators inherit the amplitude boost. Stress enhancement may thus be partially pre-linguistic — a "coordinative root" of stress — with diachronic phonologisation (Hyman 1976) converting the coupling effect into grammatical prominence, possibly amplified later by µT-gesture clock-slowing in stress languages. Caveats: acoustic rather than articulatory evidence for jaw movement; some f0/intensity patterns language-specific and unexplained; a single task type; Medʉmba data limited by small lexicon.

Relevance to Calliope-TS

This paper targets the deepest question underneath any scansion tool — what is stress, and where does it come from? — with several concrete implications:

  • Insight: duration is the primitive stress correlate. Gordon & Roettger's (2017) survey (cited here) shows duration is the most robust cross-linguistic stress cue (85%); this paper shows it is also the one that emerges from pure coordination. For Calliope, whose meter fitter scores duration-adjacent properties through syllable weight and stress, this justifies the primacy of weight/duration-based cues over pitch in the English default — and explains why the toolkit's stress keys read naturally even without any f0 machinery.
  • Insight: text-setting is not arbitrary. Calliope's ambition to handle song lyrics and text-setting is directly supported: stressed syllables map to musical strong beats because both are coordination anchors; the tool's meter identification for lyric corpora (e.g. the Beatles examples in the project's own context notes) is grounded in the same coupling principle this paper documents experimentally.
  • Insight: prominence without stress (tone languages). Medʉmba's stem-initial positional prominence attracts the beat exactly like English stress — support for Calliope's non-essentialising stance on ternary meters and non-English prosodies: the kit's "Worldish" English-translation ambitions should treat beat-attraction (positional/tonal prominence) as a legitimate analog of stress for meter-fitting, which the accentual/dolnik layer already effectively does.
  • Insight: enhancement is anchored, not global. The localisation of effects to beat-aligned syllables mirrors Calliope's key-stress philosophy (right edges of units as the reliable evidence): enhancement lives at anchored positions; a fitter should concentrate its evidence there — which the key-stress weighting already implements.
  • Insight for the reading/teaching use case: if "speaking to a beat" naturally produces the cues of stress, then Calliope's reading-aid function (key-stress-coloured output for recitation) is teaching the motor system's own language — the tool's contour output aligns with what a metronome-coordinated reader will actually produce.

Key named insights

  1. Coordinative roots of stress: in-phase coupling with a beat produces stress-like phonetic enhancement (duration, F1) in stress and non-stress languages alike.
  2. Enhancement is anchored: only beat-aligned syllables are enhanced — the effect is positional, not global.
  3. Duration as the primitive: the coupling effect shows up exactly in the cue that is most robustly diagnostic of stress cross-linguistically.
  4. Phonologisation pathway: coupling-driven lengthening is a plausible diachronic precursor to grammatical stress (via enhancement under in-phase coordination).
