Linking gestural representations to syllable count judgments: A cross-language test

Anisia Popescu & Ioana Chitoran — Laboratory Phonology: Journal of the Association for Laboratory Phonology, 13(1), 1–48 (2022). DOI: 10.16995/labphon.7681
The gist. Some English monosyllables are "sesquisyllables" — words like feel, file, fire, beer (tense vowel/diphthong + liquid coda) get variable syllable-count judgments (1, 1.5, or 2). This paper argues the cause is gestural structure, not duration: English coda liquids (dark [ɫ], rhotic /ɹ/) contain an early, vocalic tongue-dorsum gesture timed before the consonantal tongue-tip gesture, so a sesquisyllable rime contains three sequentially coordinated vocalic gestures (nucleus, offglide, liquid-TD) — and speakers' intuitions count them. German, with the same word shapes and vowel-length contrast but a clear [l] whose gestures are synchronous, shows no sesquisyllable judgments and no coda-type effect in reaction times. Production data confirm that acoustic duration alone does not explain the judgments (the longest rimes were /eɪdʒ/ — not liquid rimes — and got zero over-one judgments). Model: "over-one SCJs are attributed to any rime which has more than two sequentially coordinated vocalic gestures."

What the paper does

Parallel experiments in American English (18 speakers) and German (16 speakers): (1) a production task (words in carrier phrases; rime durations measured, normalised), immediately followed by (2) a syllable count judgment (SCJ) task with three response options (1 / 1.5 / 2 syllables), plus reaction-time recording. Stimuli: tense vowels and diphthongs × coda types (open, stop, nasal, lateral, rhotic), phonetically controlled.

Key findings

  • English: over-one SCJs occurred only for liquid-coda rimes; diphthongs outranked tense vowels (diphthong + liquid = the strongest sesquisyllable class); rhotics outranked laterals (e.g. liar/fire got the most 2-syllable judgments); reaction times were significantly longer for liquid rimes even when speakers ultimately chose "1" — processing reflects the ambiguity.
  • German: no coda-type pattern in SCJs (over-one judgments scattered randomly across all coda types and even open syllables); no reaction-time difference; "2" was never chosen for target monosyllables.
  • Duration: not a good predictor across rime types (longest rime = stop coda /eɪdʒ/, zero over-one judgments; /uːk/≈/uːl/ durations but very different judgments). Within the liquid class, longer rimes did correlate with higher judgments — duration is a by-product of the gestural configuration, not the cause.
  • Model: assumes diphthongs = 2 vocalic gestures (different constriction locations), tense vowels ≈ 2 (similar locations, more overlap), liquid TD gestures = vocalic and sequentially timed; over-one SCJs arise when >2 vocalic gestures are sequentially coordinated. Predicts German (synchronous gestures) has no sesquisyllables; predicts Dutch dialects with dark coda /l/ as a testing ground.

Interpretation & caveats

The study extends Tilsen & Cohn (2016) cross-linguistically and replaces their duration-based account with a gestural one in the Articulatory Phonology / coupled-oscillator frame. Caveats: acoustic (not articulatory) data; the German clear-/l/ gestural specification is assumed, not measured; the model is limited to sesquisyllables (doesn't yet handle other weight/phonotactic phenomena); SCJs are metalinguistic and task-sensitive, though the cross-language contrast argues the task is informative.

Relevance to Calliope-TS

Syllable counting is the humblest and most load-bearing step in any scansion pipeline, and Calliope has to count syllables for poetry where exactly these words are everywhere (fire, hour, our, power, desire, choir, world, pearl…):

  • Insight: syllable-count ambiguity is real, structured, and phonological, not orthographic. The sesquisyllable class (tense vowel/diphthong + liquid coda) is precisely the zone where Calliope's elision/synaeresis machinery and the CMU syllable counts will disagree with readers' intuitions. This paper explains why and gives the principled class: words like fire/liar are 1.5 syllables to many native intuitions, and a tool that forces 1 or 2 will mis-scan lines (e.g. in iambic contexts where poets exploit exactly this ambiguity — "the fire of thine eyes" style rhythm).
  • Insight: weight is not (only) duration. The rime-weight columns in Nounsing-Pro's augmented CMU (finalWeight, H/L, coda geometry) are static; this paper shows weight-adjacent intuitions track gestural structure. For poetry, words like fire arguably carry "extra" weight in the rime that a duration-blind phonemic count misses — a candidate refinement for syllable-weight features used in rhyme and meter logic.
  • Insight: reaction time as ambiguity signal. The English RT effect (longer decision times for liquid rimes even when the answer is "1") suggests a confidence/ambiguity signal the toolkit could emulate — e.g. flagging sesquisyllable-class line-ends in the web app as metrically ambiguous (the fitter already returns near-tie rankings; this paper names a systematic source of such ties).
  • Insight: language-specific syllabification defaults. German's lack of sesquisyllables is a reminder that syllable-count intuitions are dialect/language-specific; Calliope's Russian and future-language pipelines need their own counts (the Russian pipeline's syllable counting from stress dictionaries already is one).
  • Direct use-case: for the rewrite module (stress-preserving rewrites), the sesquisyllable class is a natural constraint family — words that behave as 1.5 syllables should be substitutable with each other, not with strict mono- or disyllables.

Key named insights

  1. The tri-vocalic rime: >2 sequentially coordinated vocalic gestures in the rime ⇒ sesquisyllabic intuitions — a gestural criterion for a classic metrical puzzle.
  2. Duration is a symptom: acoustic length correlates with judgments only within the sesquisyllable class; across classes it predicts nothing.
  3. The liquid-coda signature: dark [ɫ]/rhotic codas are special in English in exactly the words poets exploit (fire/hour/power); German clear [l] shows the contrast cleanly.
  4. Judgment-vs-processing dissociation: longer RTs for ambiguous rimes even with "1" answers — ambiguity is measurable before the verdict.
