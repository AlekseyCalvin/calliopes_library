Hierarchical distinctions in the production and perception of nuclear tunes in American English

Jennifer Cole, Jeremy Steffman, Stefanie Shattuck-Hufnagel & Sam Tilsen — Laboratory Phonology: Journal of the Association for Laboratory Phonology, 14(1), 1–51 (2023). DOI: 10.16995/labphon.9437
The gist. The Autosegmental-Metrical (AM) model of intonation predicts 8 phonologically distinct phrase-final "nuclear tunes" from combining 2 pitch accents (H*, L*) × 2 phrase accents (H-, L-) × 2 boundary tones (H%, L%). Using imitation (production) and AX discrimination (perception) experiments, this paper shows that the full 8-way contrast does not hold up: speakers and listeners robustly distinguish only a High-Rising vs Non-High-Rising dichotomy, with ~5 emergent tune classes, and the "lost" distinctions (e.g. HHH vs HHL) differ only in final-f0 scaling that is barely discriminable. The data support a binary {H*, L*} accent contrast and at most a ternary {H%, M%, L%} boundary contrast — and no independent role for the phrase accent (H-/L-). Tune shapes are organised hierarchically, not as an unordered inventory.

What the paper does

The AM framework (Pierrehumbert 1980; Ladd 2008) is the standard model behind ToBI annotation. It claims phrase-final pitch contours are built compositionally from three tonal components, giving 2×2×2 = 8 "basic" nuclear tunes (HHH, HHL, HLH, HLL, LHH, LHL, LLH, LLL). This paper is the first large-scale, multi-method test of whether all 8 are real categories.

Method: Production — 30 American English speakers imitated resynthesised model tunes (3 trisyllabic names × 2 speakers) by transposing each melody onto a new sentence; f0 trajectories analysed with (a) RMSD accuracy/distance measures, (b) bottom-up k-means clustering over unlabelled trajectories, (c) a bidirectional-LSTM neural-net classifier trained on labelled imitations, (d) GAMM smooths and turning-point alignment. Perception — a separate 30 listeners did AX same/different discrimination on all 64 ordered tune pairs.

Key findings

  • Five emergent clusters, three of which merge a predicted tune pair: A = low-to-mid rising {LHL, LLH}; B = rise-fall {HLH, HLL}; C = high-rising {HHH, HHL}; D = low-to-high rising {LHH}; E = low-fall {LLL}. The neural net (65% accuracy, chance 12.5%) confused exactly the same pairs that clustered together.
  • The primary distinction is High-Rising vs Non-High-Rising — 27–29 of 30 speakers partitioned their data this way, and perception was near-ceiling exactly when one member of a pair was high-rising. Secondary distinctions among non-rising tunes were speaker-variable and weakly perceived.
  • Fine-grained differences persist: GAMMs found small but real final-f0 differences within merged pairs, plus a domed vs scooped rise-shape difference (HHH vs HHL) that AM target-and-interpolation cannot predict — better handled by Tonal Center of Gravity (Barnes et al. 2012, 2021).
  • Phrase accent vs boundary tone do not behave as separate contrastive units: tunes differing only in phrase accent or only in boundary tone were the ones that collapsed (e.g. {HHH,HHL} differ only in boundary tone; {HLH,HLL} only in phrase accent).
  • Imitation is not parroting: accuracy differed by model gender and speakers didn't reproduce the models' pairwise distances — they transduced categorical structure, not raw pitch detail.

Interpretation & caveats

The authors propose a hierarchy of tune distinctiveness with "attractor" tunes (Roessig, Mücke & Grice 2019): robust distinctions are context-independent; finer ones may need discourse support. Caveats they flag: resynthesised stimuli (no discourse context, identical preambles, only f0 varied — no duration/amplitude/voice-quality cues), stress-initial trisyllabic nuclear words only, and no natural-production dataset exists yet to validate the resynthesis.

Relevance to Calliope-TS

Calliope works from text, not acoustics, but its prosodic assumptions descend directly from the AM tradition, and its pipeline claims specific things about phrase-final structure ("beginnings free, endings strict"; IU/PP right-edge key stresses; caesura at strong boundaries):

  • Insight: right-edge evidence should be graded hierarchically. The empirical finding that a High-Rising/Non-High-Rising contrast is categorical while final-f0 scaling is gradient maps beautifully onto Calliope's key-stress weights (IU=3, PP=2, PW=1) — some right-edge events are simply worth more than others, and the hierarchy is not flat.
  • Insight: not every ToBI distinction is worth modelling. A text-based scansion tool cannot (and need not) reconstruct L-/H- vs L%/H% distinctions; this paper gives principled cover for collapsing them, and suggests the useful output space is roughly: rising vs falling vs low-flat tune class at phrase end.
  • Insight: tune-shape > tone-strings. The domed-vs-scooped finding supports holistic, shape-based representations of intonation (cf. the "attractor" view) rather than concatenative tone targets — a caution for any future acoustic/intonation module in the kit (e.g. TTS-driven stress verification).
  • Caution for rhyme/caesura logic: if phrase-final pitch patterns are coarser than the AM model claims, then basing caesura strength on boundary-tone identity is over-confident; positional (foot-edge) and durational evidence should carry more weight — which is exactly what Calliope's caesura module already does (foot-boundary alignment is mandatory).
  • Methodological echo: the paper's "imitation as transduction" design is a nice model for how a scansion tool could be evaluated — check that outputs recover category structure, not exact acoustic detail.

Key named insights

  1. The High-Rising Attractor: the most robust intonational contrast in American English is categorical (high-rising vs not), everything else is a gradient within it.
  2. The Collapse of Phrase Accents: H-/L- distinctions are not independently contrastive in production or perception — a direct empirical challenge to part of the AM inventory.
  3. Shape Over Targets: domed/scooped rise shapes implicate Tonal Center of Gravity — dynamic, non-linear representations — over target-and-interpolation models.
  4. Hierarchy of Distinctiveness: tune systems are organised as a two-level hierarchy, not an 8-way flat contrast; gradient detail exists but is perceptually secondary.
