Phonetic and phonological aspects of prosodic focus marking in Canadian English

Jiseung Kim & Anja Arnhold — Laboratory Phonology: Journal of the Association for Laboratory Phonology, 15(1), 1–38 (2024). DOI: 10.16995/labphon.9316
The gist. A large controlled production study (37 Western Canadian English speakers, 24 transitive SVO sentences × 4 focus conditions) showing that Canadian English marks narrow contrastive focus differently from Mainstream American English (MAE): focus shows up mainly as duration lengthening (plus intensity and f0-range effects), not as raised f0; and the most reliable signature of focus is actually weakening of focus-adjacent material (pre- and post-focal shortening, lower f0 and intensity). Pitch-accent presence/absence tracks information status strongly (focused → accented, given → de-accented), but accent type (H* vs L+H* vs L*) does not — H* dominates everything (~73% of accents), and the MAE "contrastive accent" L+H* is rare, suggesting Canadian English may not even contrast H* vs L+H*.

What the paper does

Earlier literature on English focus prosody is inconsistent about which acoustic correlates (duration, intensity, f0) mark focus, and about whether information structure is signalled by pitch-accent types. Almost all of it is on MAE or British varieties; Canadian English had been assumed "broadly representative" of English without evidence. Kim & Arnhold test this assumption directly.

Speakers produced answers to questions eliciting broad focus (BF) vs narrow contrastive focus on subject (SF), verb (VF), or object (OF). Six acoustic measures were taken per constituent (duration, relative intensity, f0 range/max/mean/min), and ToBI-trained annotators labelled pitch accents (presence and type).

Key findings

Acoustics: enhancement of the target, weakening of its neighbours

  • Focus-induced lengthening replicated for all three constituents (S +58ms, V +50ms, O +22ms vs broad focus).
  • Post-focal weakening was consistent: given verbs and objects after a focused constituent were shorter, softer, and lower in f0 (also pre-focal weakening for some measures). The authors note two possibilities: focus is marked mainly by weakening of adjacent material, or Canadian English simply lacks the across-the-board f0 enhancement reported for MAE.
  • Contrary to H1: no f0 enhancement on the focused constituent itself — focused subjects and verbs were actually lower in f0 max/mean/min than in broad focus.
  • Phrase-final constituents are progressively longer (objects > verbs > subjects) — ordinary final lengthening.

Pitch accents: presence matters, type does not

  • Accent presence rose with focus: narrow-focus constituents accented >90% of the time; broad focus less; given least.
  • H* dominated all conditions (~73% of accented constituents); L+H* was rare (~14%) and L* modest (~12%). The hypothesis that contrastive focus ⇒ L+H* (Pierrehumbert & Hirschberg 1990) was not supported.
  • L* did not mark givenness; accent position correlated better with accent type than information status did.

Interpretation & caveats

The authors cautiously conclude that Western Canadian English may (a) rely on duration rather than f0 for focus, and (b) either have a different pitch-accent inventory than MAE or lack a real H*~L+H* contrast (as has been argued for other varieties, e.g. Arvaniti & Garding 2007 for Minnesota; Ladd & Schepman 2003). Caveats: read speech, undergraduate speakers, a single regional variety, modest inter-annotator reliability (κ=0.24 on full labels; 0.49 on accent presence).

Relevance to Calliope-TS

Calliope's phrasal/prominence machinery (Wagner-inspired subordination; discourseGiven/rhymeFocal stanza passes; the focusAssociateOf, isInherentlyGiven, and contrastive-focus predicates) makes claims about how focus and givenness should behave. This paper is a strong empirical reality-check for those claims, plus a caution:

  • Insight: "focus = enhancement of target" is not universal. If Western Canadian English (and per the paper, possibly other varieties) marks focus chiefly by lengthening and by de-accenting/de-weakening the surroundings, then a pipeline that treats focus as a binary prominence boost is over-simplified. Calliope's five-tier scale already has the right shape (target up, given down); this paper justifies differential cue weighting (duration-heavy, f0-light) and supports weighting w_prom-type constraints more strongly for duration than for pitch.
  • Insight: accent presence > accent type for givenness. Calliope's tier system effectively collapses "pitch accent type" into presence/absence plus prominence rank — this paper suggests that is actually a faithful model of at least one variety of English.
  • Insight: de-accenting is the strongest signal of givenness. This validates Calliope's demoteGivenNuclei/discourseGiven design and suggests givenness demotion could be stronger than it currently is (given words were the most reliably distinguishable class in this data).
  • Caution for dialect handling: if focus prosody varies this much between MAE and Canadian English, a tool aimed at "English" broadly may need dialect-aware defaults (cf. Calliope's overridable UDPipe model and the project's "Worldish" ambitions).
  • Relevance to the meter layer: post-focal compression means phrase-final position after a focus is prosodically flatter — a subtlety relevant to Calliope's "beginnings free, endings strict" key-stress logic: an early focus pre-empts some of the strength of the right edge.

Key named insights

  1. The Weakening Signature: focus-adjacent reduction (duration/f0/intensity) may be the most reliable acoustic correlate of focus — more reliable than on-focus enhancement.
  2. H*-Dominance: in at least one North American variety, H* is the workhorse accent; L+H* is not the "contrastive accent" of the textbooks.
  3. Presence-over-Type: information status is encoded by whether an accent is there, not by which accent it is — good news for tier-based (non-ToBI) systems.
  4. Dialectal Caveat: "English focus prosody" is not one thing; empirical norms should be variety-aware.
