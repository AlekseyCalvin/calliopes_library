The voice of experience: Causal inference in phonotactic adaptation

Thomas Denby & Matthew Goldrick — Laboratory Phonology: Journal of the Association for Laboratory Phonology, 12(1), 5, 1–28 (2021). DOI: 10.5334/labphon.267
The gist. Listeners rapidly adapt to novel phonotactic constraints (which sounds may appear where in syllables), but the degree of adaptation depends on causal inference about the talker: when two speakers plausibly speak different languages (English vs French, or Hindi vs Hungarian), listeners generalise each speaker's constraint strongly; when speakers plausibly share a language (two native English speakers), adaptation is weak. Across three experiments (640+ listeners), the pooled effect is ~2×: a ~18.7% "legality advantage" for different-language talker pairs vs ~9.9% for same-language pairs. This supports the rational learner framework (Kleinschmidt & Jaeger 2015): prior experience with the structure of variation — languages differ in phonotactics, individuals within a dialect mostly don't — constrains what you learn from whom.

What the paper does

Three preregistered experiments using a recognition-memory phonotactic-learning paradigm (Bernard 2015): listeners hear syllable sequences from two "talkers," each subject to a different constraint (e.g. Talker A's codas are fricatives, Talker B's are stops), then must judge whether novel syllables were heard before. A "legality advantage" (higher false-alarm rate on constraint-following novel items) indicates the constraint was learned. The twist: the two talkers' language backgrounds are manipulated — same native language, same non-native language, or different languages, with weak vs strong phonetic cues to background.

Key findings

  • Different backgrounds → strong adaptation; shared backgrounds → weak adaptation. English listeners adapted more to talker-specific constraints when the talkers were (perceived as) speakers of different languages — even two non-native languages (Hindi vs Hungarian) — showing fine-grained distinctions among non-native grammars, not just native-vs-foreign.
  • Cue strength to language background mattered less than expected (weak [u] vs strong [y] French vowel cues produced the same adaptation) — any reliable signal of "different languages" sufficed.
  • Surprising mid-case: two French (non-native, shared) talkers produced moderate adaptation — listeners are less sure two non-native speakers share a grammar than two natives do, because their knowledge of the non-native phonetics is sparser.
  • L2 experience didn't matter: self-reported bilingualism/early-L2 did not modulate the effects — even modest exposure teaches the "different languages have different phonotactics" prior.
  • Learning still occurred in same-language conditions (weaker), showing bottom-up pattern evidence can partially override the prior (contra Onishi et al. 2002).

Interpretation & caveats

The authors situate the work in the rational-learner/hierarchical-indexical account of adaptation: variation is attributed to sources (language → dialect → speaker) on the basis of prior structure, and adaptation targets the attributed source. Caveats: individual talker idiosyncrasies mattered (one anomalous talker shifted results), the weak/strong cue manipulation used a narrow phonetic contrast, and explicit beliefs about talkers were never probed.

Relevance to Calliope-TS

This is a learning/cognition paper, not a prosody paper — but it speaks to three things a poetry-scansion toolkit quietly depends on: how much to trust per-speaker or per-dialect regularity, how gradient phonotactic knowledge interacts with categorical grammar, and the epistemological status of "lab languages":

  • Insight: prior structure controls generalization. For Calliope's language detection and per-language defaults, this is a direct argument for modelling "Englishness" as a graded prior rather than a fixed grammar: the tool's English rules should be strongest exactly where variation across Englishes is smallest (core phonotactics), and weakest where dialectal variation is real (focus prosody — cf. the Kim & Arnhold paper in this set).
  • Insight: dictionary-vs-rule decisions are attribution problems. When Calliope assigns stress to an out-of-vocabulary word, it is implicitly asking "is this variance lexical (store it) or systematic (rule it)?" The rational-learner framing suggests a principled answer: rules for variance shared across speakers (phonotactics, stress windows), lexicon for variance that is word-specific — which is exactly Calliope's current architecture (CMU lexicon + OOV rules), now with a cognitive justification.
  • Insight: analogy for corpus tuning. The "lab language" finding (Warker 2013: experimental constraints persist across a week of real English) has an engineering echo: a scansion tool tuned on a poetry corpus is learning a "lab language"; adaptation to it should be slow and source-aware — a caution against overfitting rule systems to one poet's/corpus's statistics (very much in the spirit of Calliope's standing law that no categorical rule may rest on broad POS/lemma classes).
  • Insight: probabilistic weighting beats categorical flags. The legality-advantage methodology is a clean template for evaluating whether a candidate constraint (e.g. a new clash-resolution rule) is genuinely learned/generalised by a system across contexts, not just applied to the items it was written for.

Key named insights

  1. Attribution-before-adaptation: learning is gated by whom you think the variation belongs to — a general principle for any system that tunes itself to a corpus or dialect.
  2. Shared-grammar prior: same-dialect talkers are assumed to share a grammar unless the signal says otherwise; the prior is weaker for non-native pairs.
  3. Phonotactics as the deep layer: cross-language phonotactic variation is large and systematic; within-dialect variation is minimal — the ideal substrate for source-attribution heuristics.
  4. Legality-advantage as an evaluation metric: a behavioural assay for whether a constraint was abstracted (generalised to novel items) vs memorised.
