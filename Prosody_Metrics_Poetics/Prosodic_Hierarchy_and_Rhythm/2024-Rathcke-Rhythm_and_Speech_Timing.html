Tapping into linguistic rhythm

Tamara Rathcke, Chia-Yuan Lin, Simone Falk & Simone Dalla Bella — Laboratory Phonology: Journal of the Association for Laboratory Phonology, 12(1), 11, 1–32 (2021). DOI: 10.5334/labphon.248
The gist. After decades of inconclusive acoustic measurements (no isochrony was ever found in speech), this paper argues rhythm should be studied by movement: using sensorimotor-synchronisation (SMS) finger tapping to looped spoken sentences, 29 English listeners did entrain to natural language. Synchronisation reliably anchored on vowel onsets (the best of five tested landmarks — better than syllable onsets or energy/amplitude maxima), was more accurate on metrically strong and accented vowels (weak vowels drew fewer taps), and mimicked metronome-tapping signatures (negative mean asynchrony/anticipation, reduced with practice, musicality helping). A non-synchronised motor-reproduction task showed weaker period tracking, drifting toward individual spontaneous tapping rates. Conclusion: natural language can entrain movement; beat perception in English locks onto vowels and the metrical structure of the utterance; movement-based paradigms are a valid, ecologically-grounded window into linguistic rhythm (the "speech-to-song illusion" was controlled for and was not necessary for entrainment).

What the paper does

Two tasks on six SBE sentences (4-, 7-, 10-syllable; balanced for speech-to-song likelihood): SMS — tap along with the perceived beat of a 20× looped sentence; NMR — listen to 10 loops, then reproduce the beat pattern from memory. Acoustic anchors compared: syllable onset, vowel onset, local amplitude maximum (LAM), energy maximum (maxE), fastest energy rise (maxD ≈ p-centre proxy). Individual musicality (BAASTA battery) and spontaneous tapping rates measured; density-peak extraction over aggregated taps; mixed models on asynchrony, tap probability, anticipation, and inter-tap-interval (ITI) period tracking.

Key findings

  • Vowel onsets are the SMS anchor: smallest absolute asynchronies vs vowel onsets (significantly better than syllable onsets and maxE; statistically tied with LAM and maxD but numerically best).
  • Metrical sensitivity: taps were more likely near metrically strong (z=3.28) and accented (z=4.50) vowels than weak ones — the tapping itself recovers the stress pattern (cf. Allen 1972: "tap before the nuclear vowels of rhythmically accented syllables").
  • Anticipation: negative mean asynchrony, largest sentence-initially, decreasing with serial position (accuracy improves over the sentence); shorter rise-times and steeper rise-slopes were anticipated more.
  • Practice and individual differences: most participants entrained by repetition 3, stabilising after ~5 tapping cycles; musicality improved accuracy; confidence ratings didn't predict performance.
  • NMR is weaker: period tracking present (ITI scaled with vocalic intervals) but partially swamped by spontaneous tapping tempo, with more regularised (lower-variability) output.
  • S2S control: high- vs low-speech-to-song sentences behaved the same in accuracy (only tap count differed) — the illusion is not required for linguistic entrainment.

Interpretation & caveats

The authors propose that beat perception in language is a constructive perceptual process built on the prosodic system (stress alternations; Liberman & Prince 1977), anchored at vowel nuclei — consistent with drummed-language evidence (Amazonian Bora: inter-vocalic intervals as rhythmic units) and with sonority-based accounts of speech segmentation. SMS with language behaves like SMS with metronomes/music: the same timing mechanisms subserve both. Caveats: English only; clear read speech; 29 participants; vowel-onset anchoring may be English-specific (cross-linguistic extension is future work); acoustic measures (rise-time) are composite and hard to interpret.

Relevance to Calliope-TS

This paper is methodological and perceptual, but its findings bear directly on what Calliope's meter layer is trying to recover and how it should weight evidence:

  • Insight: vowel onsets are the perceptual beat locus. Calliope's meter fitter works syllable-by-syllable, but the perceptual "moment of occurrence" of a beat is the vowel onset (p-centre logic). For the fitter's alignment-sensitive scoring (and for the future acoustic/verification modules), anchoring beats at vowel nuclei rather than syllable onsets is the empirically right choice — and for the display layer, it suggests that the visual scansion should emphasise vowel nuclei as the beat carriers.
  • Insight: metrical strength is recoverable from the surface rhythm alone. The fact that tapping recovers stress/accent positions from looped natural speech is strong evidence that the information Calliope computes (stress tiers from syntax+lexicon) is also in the signal — validating the phonological-scansion approach against a perceptual gold standard: if human entrainment recovers the same beat structure, the tool's key-stress output is converging on a real, shared representation.
  • Insight: "accentual" perception is hierarchical. Taps landed on accented > strong > weak vowels — a three-level hierarchy matching Calliope's tier ladder (s/m/n vs w/x) and its key-stress weights. The accent-vs-stress distinction (phrasal accent over metrical strength) maps to the toolkit's two-layer design (lexical contour vs phrasal tier).
  • Insight for the dolnik/accentual layer: the paper's ITI period-tracking results (vocalic intervals as the period) support Gasparov-style accentual analysis: in dolnik/accentual verse the relevant periodicity is between beats (vocalic anchors), not between syllables — Calliope's ictus-interval machinery (applyRhythmLayer) is on the right track, and this paper supplies the perceptual grounding for why inter-ictus intervals should be measured at vowel nuclei.
  • Insight: entrainment/looping as an evaluation paradigm. The synchronisation task is a natural evaluation instrument for the kit's stress keys: generate speech from the tool's predicted contours (TTS) and measure human tapping alignment — a perceptual validation loop for scansion quality that goes beyond corpus accuracy.

Key named insights

  1. Vowels anchor the beat: English beat perception locks onto vowel onsets — the p-centre question resolves, for language, in favour of vocalic nuclei.
  2. Language entrains movement: natural, unmanipulated speech supports SMS with metronome-like signatures — rhythm is real, just not isochronous.
  3. Metrical hierarchy in perception: accent > stress > weak recover a three-level prominence structure from raw acoustics.
  4. Repetition reveals structure: looping strips away lexical/semantic load and exposes prosodic organisation — a principle with direct experimental (and computational) applications.
