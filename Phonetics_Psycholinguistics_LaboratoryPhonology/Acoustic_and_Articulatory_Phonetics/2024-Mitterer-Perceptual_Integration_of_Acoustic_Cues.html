Adventures in /ʔ/

Holger Mitterer — Laboratory Phonology: Journal of the Association for Laboratory Phonology, 17(1), 1–29 (2026). DOI: 10.16995/labphon.18702
The gist. A review-plus-programme paper arguing that the glottal stop is a uniquely revealing "weak phoneme" that adjudicates core theoretical disputes in Laboratory Phonology. The counterintuitive headline: the glottal stop behaves surprisingly like a full phoneme in German — where almost everyone assumes it isn't one — and surprisingly weakly like a phoneme in Maltese, where it demonstrably is one. The conclusion: the glottal stop is always a weak phoneme (it constrains lexical access less than oral stops even where phonemic — Maltese listeners activate qattus when hearing vowel-initial attur), and the threshold for granting it phonemic status should be low. Along the way: deletion of an orthographically-uncoded German glottal stop hurts lexical access exactly as much as deletion of a coded segment (orthography doesn't drive implicit speech processing); L2 (Italian) learners acquire German glottal-stop production implicitly without any teaching; Maltese epenthetic glottal stops are stored in the lexicon (multiple form representations); and segmental information (an epenthetic glottal stop on u 'and') can cue prosodic parsing (boundary placement), demonstrating segmental–suprasegmental cross-talk.

What the paper does

Not one experiment but a synthesis of a decade of the author's work: German glottal-stop deletion and lexical access (visual-world; Mitterer & Reinisch 2015), explicit-rating orthography effects, Italian-L2 acquisition of German /h/ vs /ʔ/ (Eger et al. 2019), Maltese geminate vs singleton glottal stops (production durations + 2AFC perception continua; Mitterer 2018), Maltese underlying-vs-epenthetic glottal stops (production study; gating; visual-world with pseudo-onset-overlap pairs qattus–attur; speaker-adaptation failure; Mitterer, Kim & Cho 2019, 2021a,b), and prosodic-boundary inference from epenthetic glottal stops in Maltese coordinate structures (Daniel jew Gordon u Malcolm).

Key findings

  • German /ʔ/ ≈ /h/ in lexical access: deleting either hurts recognition equally in a visual-world task, despite /h/ being orthographically coded and /ʔ/ not — orthography affects explicit ratings only, not implicit processing. This removes the main argument for non-phonemic status.
  • Implicit L2 learning: Italian learners produced German glottal stops ~65% correctly (vs ~75% for /h/) despite zero instruction and zero orthographic support, and their explicit ratings distinguished correct from substituted forms — but their online word recognition ignored /h/↔/ʔ/ substitutions (a perception-production dissociation).
  • Maltese /ʔ/ is a weak phoneme: even in Maltese, where /ʔ/ is unrestricted, phonemic, and written <q>, listeners show onset-overlap competition between glottal-stop-initial and vowel-initial words (qattus–attur) — competition not found for /t/-initial pairs (tabib–abjad). Glottal stops don't constrain lexical access as oral stops do.
  • Epenthetic /ʔ/ is represented: Maltese vowel-initial words are stored with glottal-stop-initial variants too (multiple form representations); the boundary-lengthening cue to "epenthetic" status affects late (gating) but not early (eye-tracking) processing.
  • Segmental→prosodic inference: an epenthetic glottal stop before u 'and' biases listeners toward a prosodic boundary before it — segmental detail guides prosodic structure (and syntactic parse choice).
  • Geminates: glottal-stop gemination is cued by closure (80%+ full closures in geminates vs glottalisation in singletons), and listeners use these secondary cues, not just duration — supporting cue-based, not featural, geminate representations.

Interpretation & caveats

None of Hall's (2013) "weak phoneme" criteria (predictability, foreign stratum, variability, low frequency) fit the Maltese glottal stop — it is frequent and free — so its weakness must be inherent: vowels outweigh consonants, and glottal stops are the weakest consonants (cf. the vowel-mutability literature, van Ooijen 1996). Caveats: mostly single-author programme; some findings rest on small L2 samples; the German claim remains controversial by design.

Relevance to Calliope-TS

The glottal stop is a real presence in English verse — as hiatus-breaker, elision marker, and word-initial glottalisation — and this paper speaks to several concrete places in the Calliope pipeline:

  • Insight: glottalisation is a prominence/boundary cue worth representing. Steffman (2023) and the English literature show vowel-initial glottalisation marks prosodic boundaries and prominence. For a text-based tool, the orthographic reflexes are limited, but the paper's Maltese result — segmental detail cues prosodic parsing — is a general principle: Calliope's dash/comma → boundary and elision → syllable decisions are exactly the kind of segmental-detail-to-prosody mapping that listeners make; making the tool's boundary decisions visible (caesura module) is empirically grounded.
  • Insight: multiple form representations. Mitterer's Maltese conclusion (vowel-initial words are stored with and without the glottal stop) parallels Calliope's handling of poetic elision variants: o'er as one syllable, heav'n reduced — the toolkit already effectively stores multiple forms (dictionary + elided variants). This paper says that's not just a metrical convenience; it's how the mental lexicon works.
  • Insight: weak segments should have weak constraint weight. If glottal stops (and by analogy schwa) constrain lexical access weakly, then syllable-count and stress decisions hinging on them are intrinsically low-confidence — relevant to Calliope's OOV handling of apostrophised forms ('tis, 'twas: aphaeresis clitics) and to the fitter's treatment of reduced clitics (x-tier) on beats: those positions are weak by nature, and the penalties should be proportionate (they already are — x on a beat is the cardinal violation, but promotion exists).
  • Insight: orthography ≠ phonology in implicit processing. Calliope is a text-in tool, but this paper warns against letting spelling drive prosody where the spoken form diverges (silent letters, hiatus, glottalised vowels) — a caution for syllable counting from orthography and for rhyme classification (eye-rhyme vs true rhyme), where Calliope already correctly goes through phonemes.
  • Insight for the Russian side: the Russian pipeline handles ёфикация and stress dictionaries; glottal-ish phenomena (vowel-initial word boundaries, hiatus) are handled by the accentology layer — the "weak segment" framing transfers as a general principle for any language's reduced/unstable segments.

Key named insights

  1. The inherently weak phoneme: glottal stops never constrain lexical access like oral stops, even when fully phonemic — weakness is intrinsic, so the phonemicity threshold should be low.
  2. Orthography-free implicit processing: deleting an uncoded segment hurts recognition as much as deleting a coded one; orthography shapes only explicit judgments.
  3. Segmental-detail → prosodic-parsing: listeners use segmental cues (epenthetic glottal stops) to infer prosodic boundaries — the two processing streams talk to each other.
  4. Multiple forms in the lexicon: words that alternate (with/without glottal stop, with/without schwa) are stored multiply — a representational pluralism that text-based tools can mirror.
